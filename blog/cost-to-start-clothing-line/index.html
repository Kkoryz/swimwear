How much money do you need to start a clothing line?

By Wenjie DengPublished 17 November 202611 min read

To start a clothing line, you need enough cash to develop your products, get inventory delivered, launch sales, and pay the bills until customer receipts can fund operations and replenishment. For a sense of scale, Erply's 2025 guide to clothing line startup costs budgets **$5,000–$15,000** for print-on-demand or dropshipping with basic branding and limited marketing, and **$15,000–$50,000** for small-batch production with professional branding and a comprehensive marketing launch.

Your clothing line startup budget, broken down

Pick your production route before you pick a savings target. Those published budgets describe different kinds of businesses, and your own target comes from the products, services, and payment dates in your plan. Below, we'll walk through a swimwear collection from its sample charges to its first reorder, keeping the same assumptions all the way through.

RouteWhat needs fundingDecide before you budget
Print-on-demandArtwork, product checks, store setup, and customer acquisition; inventory investment is limitedDoes an available product suit your idea?
Existing-style private labelSelected inventory, branding, delivery, and selling costsCan the existing fit and available assortment serve your customer?
Custom manufacturingDesign, physical samples, revisions, production, delivery, and selling costsIs a distinct fit or construction essential to the offer?

The route comparison follows Erply's 2025 guide to clothing line startup costs. Collection size and how you sell also shape the budget. An online launch still needs selling infrastructure and marketing, as VistaPrint's How Much Does It Cost to Start a Clothing Brand? explains.

For swimwear, start by deciding whether an existing silhouette gives your customer the coverage and support they need. Branding can personalize a ready design, but changing the fit can mean new development. We offer both existing designs and custom development. To define the product you'd like priced, see our Bikini Manufacturer page.

Build the launch ledger around a specific collection

Give every expense its own place in the ledger, and keep the specification right beside it: fabric, lining, hardware, branding, packing, quantities by size, destination, and delivery terms. Compare the original estimate with the final payment or remaining allowance, and note why it changed. That makes it easier to tell whether a lower quote is a real saving or a different product.

Our worked example assumes a US direct-to-consumer swimwear launch. It applies our development fees and production payment terms to assumed garment prices, delivery charges, independent service costs, and sales. Use its structure to build your own budget, swapping the planning inputs for your itemized quotes.

Say the order is confirmed on April 13, 2026: 150 navy triangle tops at $12 each and 150 matching bottoms at $8 each. They sell as separates, and the sales model groups them into 150 paired purchases. The specification uses standard nylon-spandex fabric, matching lining, standard unbranded hardware, and plain individual bags. Outer presentation packaging comes from an independent supplier and gets its own budget line. All amounts are in USD.

The assumed factory garment bill is $3,000. Outer packaging brings the inventory subtotal to $3,300, and freight and clearance bring delivered inventory to $4,200. Development and other launch expenses add $2,600, so full launch spending comes to **$6,800** against an original estimate of $6,500. The $300 increase breaks down as $100 more for packaging, $100 for sampling, and $50 each for freight and clearance.

This launch subtotal stops once the collection is ready to sell. Recurring software, fulfillment, customer acquisition, refunds, and replenishment go into the cash calendar or the sale calculation below. Founder living costs are a separate decision. Keep the import allowance tied to the shipment, and replace it with the applicable bill once you have it.

We offer FOB, CIF, and DDP shipping terms. With DDP, our all-in delivery price covers freight, duties, and customs to your door. Read the inclusions before you move a quote into your worksheet. If delivery charges are already included, enter that delivered price and don't add the same freight and clearance costs a second time.

Fund sampling through final fit approval

Our **$50 design fee per design** covers its pattern or print in all colorways, including the tech pack and CAD pattern we prepare. Each physical sample costs **$50**, plus **$50 for sample courier delivery**. These charges aren't refunded or credited against bulk production. Physical fit revisions you request are chargeable, while minor digital adjustments are free. If a sample differs significantly from the approved handover, we remake it at no charge. For what's included, see our Development page.

Write your fit brief before you pay for samples, covering intended coverage, support, measurements, fabric, lining, and movement. We assess fit against the measurement sheet and check wet stretch, coverage, and opacity before grading. In the assumed development log below, the buyer changes the coverage brief after the initial samples. Recording that reason explains why the revision is charged.

The assumed development period runs from March 2 through April 10, 2026, with $400 spent on design, samples, and courier deliveries. If you want room for one more buyer-requested physical revision with its own courier shipment, hold another $100. That reserve stays unspent in the base plan and sits outside the $6,800 launch total.

Keep the approval evidence with the expense record: the measurement sheet, dry and wet fit photographs, the requested change, and the final written approval. Compare the same garment areas under similar conditions so you can see what improved. If your product needs independent laboratory testing, add the quoted fee and time to the plan before you commit to production.

Check the lining and edge seams as part of that review. Look at how the layers meet, how the seam feels under stretch, and whether the lining settles back into place after release. These details tell you whether the sample is ready to become the production reference.

Price the assortment by style, color, and size

Our starting minimum is **50 pieces per style in each color**, split across **3 to 4 sizes**, usually in an S:M:L:XL ratio of **1:2:2:1**. The exact minimum depends on your fabric and print choices. A bikini top supplied with its bottom counts as one piece, while tops and bottoms ordered as separates count separately.

The worksheet orders separates and assumes the size allocation shown. Put the accepted allocation on the purchase order, along with the style/color minimum and any fabric or trim minimum. Confirm any total-order requirement while you're getting quotes. For more on ordering, see our FAQ.

This assumed order contains 300 garments, forming 150 same-size pairs with no unmatched opening stock. Each style is above our 50-piece starting minimum in its color. That pairing capacity depends on the allocation, though. If customers choose different sizes for tops and bottoms, some sizes can run short even when total garment quantities match.

To add a color, assume another 60 tops and 60 bottoms, each allocated S/M/L/XL as 10/20/20/10. At the same garment prices, inventory goes up by $1,200. A sample of each garment with separate courier deliveries adds $200, which brings the added commitment to $1,400 before extra packaging and delivery. Adding a colorway to these existing designs carries no new design fee.

To add a navy top style instead, assume 60 tops at $12, allocated 10/20/20/10. That adds $720 of inventory and $150 for design, a sample, and its courier delivery, for $870 before packaging and delivery. If you keep the original bottoms, paired purchases stop at 150, and 60 tops are left unmatched if everyone buys a pair.

Use these comparisons when you trim the collection to fit your cash. Adding a color repeats the commitment across matching garments. Adding a top gives customers another choice but draws on the same bottom inventory. Separates can sell independently, so base your buying plan on how you expect customers to shop.

Schedule cash payments through the first reorder

Our production payment terms are **50% at order confirmation and 50% before shipment**. Standard samples are ready in **10 to 15 days**. Bulk production usually takes **35 to 40 days** after sample approval and receipt of the deposit, depending on order size and season. Repeats of approved styles skip sampling and use the same bulk window. Put the deposit and balance for both orders in your calendar.

Our photography, branding, and store services are quoted and invoiced separately from production, with half paid when you book and half on delivery of the finished work. This example uses independent-provider costs and assumes they're paid before launch. If we supply those services, use their separate quotes and payment milestones. See our Swimwear Launch Services page.

The assumed calendar covers March 2 through August 31, 2026, including a repeat order identical to the first. Overhead is $200 a month from April through August. Base sales are 60 paired purchases in June, 30 in July, and 30 in August. Each leaves $68 after variable selling outflows, with cleared payouts available at month-end. The sale calculation below shows how that receipt is built up.

Read the cumulative deficit as the cash you, as the founder, have to put in after each event. We assume overhead and supplier bills leave the account before that month's sales payout arrives. Refunds and other variable selling costs are already deducted from the receipts. Keeping payments and receipts in this order reveals a shortfall that monthly net totals could hide.

The largest deficit is **$7,400 on June 1**, and the deficit left at the end is $3,840.

Add any founder draw on the dates you plan to take it, then recalculate the largest deficit. Treat an emergency reserve as available cash until it's spent. When you use it, record the actual payment in the calendar. Counting both the reserve and that payment as expenses would inflate your funding requirement.

Check how slower sales and delayed delivery change the target

Test the same collection against different receipt schedules. These assumed scenarios keep the reorder and overhead commitments unchanged. They show the cash you'd need if you go ahead with that replenishment plan despite slower sales. If you decide to postpone or reduce the reorder, that needs its own revised calendar.

Slow sales raise the assumed peak funding need by $1,880 over the base case, and delayed delivery raises it by $2,560. Covering the highest modeled deficit of $9,960 plus the optional $100 sampling reserve gives you a savings target of **$10,060**, before founder living costs.

The delayed-delivery case shifts stock availability and customer receipts but leaves supplier payments where they are. Add extra freight, storage, or refunds if your revised plan incurs them. If a production delay also changes the balance payment date, move that payment too. A useful stress test follows what actually changes in the order and sales schedule.

Calculate both break-even sales and cash recovery

Contribution measures what each sale leaves after product cost and variable selling expenses. You reach operating break-even when that contribution covers fixed expenses. Cash recovery asks a different question: have cleared receipts paid back the money you've already spent, including inventory still waiting to sell? Use the same order and selling assumptions to calculate each one.

Assume a paired retail purchase of $100. Allow $5 for markdowns and $5 for refunds, then deduct $3 in payment fees, $7 for fulfillment and outbound shipping, $10 for customer acquisition, and $2 for defect-related service. That leaves **$68 per paired purchase before product cost**. Delivered inventory costs $4,200 across 150 pairs, or $28 per pair, which leaves **$40 contribution per paired sale**.

The refund allowance assumes refunded goods bring in no resale proceeds during the modeled period. Defect-related service costs also get no assumed reimbursement from the supplier. If your actual refunds leave the account separately from payouts, give them their own calendar entries and adjust net receipts to match. Keep fixed launch creative separate from the advertising cost assigned to each purchase.

In this example, launch-only operating break-even is $2,600 in fixed expenses divided by $40 contribution, or **65 paired sales**. Add the modeled $1,000 overhead and fixed expenses reach $3,600, which takes **90 paired sales**. Product cost enters this calculation only as the related garments sell.

Recovering the launch cash and overhead takes $7,800 divided by $68 in net receipts, rounded up to **115 paired sales**. At 120 paired sales, receipts of $8,160 exceed that initial spending by $360. At 90 paired sales, receipts are $6,120 and $1,680 is still unrecovered, even though the model has reached operating break-even.

Once the repeat order is fully paid, the cash you've committed rises to $12,000. Recovering it at $68 per paired purchase takes **177 paired sales**. The first collection has only 150 pairs, so selling all of it still leaves $1,800 unrecovered. Under these assumptions, sales from the reorder have to close that gap.

For cash recovery, count the inventory payment when it leaves your account, and use sales receipts before product cost. Deducting product cost again would count the purchase twice. For contribution, assign product cost to each sale even though the supplier has already been paid. Keeping the two apart lets you judge both your margin and the cash available for replenishment.

Set your clothing line budget before placing an order

Set your clothing line budget by comparing the cash you have available with the largest modeled shortfall through the first reorder. If the plan doesn't fit, scale back the assortment, reconsider the production route, or revise the launch and reorder schedule before you commit.

Bring a product brief that names your styles, fabric, colors, sizes, quantities, delivery destination, and intended launch timing. Ask for itemized development, garment, branding, packing, and delivery costs, then attach payment dates to the quote. Keep your website, photography, marketing, and living-cost needs in view alongside it.

A factory quote gives you part of the answer. Your budget is complete when the collection can be developed, delivered, sold, and replenished without relying on sales cash that hasn't cleared yet.
